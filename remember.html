How he was treated

The silencing

White cadets imposed what they called silencing: they would not speak to him or socialize with him outside of official duty. It lasted his entire four years. He ate alone, studied alone, and marched among people who acted as though he were not there.

He roomed at first with James Webster Smith, the first Black cadet appointed to West Point in 1870. Smith was hazed, physically abused, and eventually dismissed. After Smith left, Flipper lived alone. Officers and professors generally treated him fairly on academic and official matters, but the peer environment stayed hostile. Contemporary accounts called it a kind of social martyrdom.

Almost the only ordinary human contact available to him came from staff — the barber, the commissary clerk — people who simply spoke to him like a person.

no society for me to enjoy — no friends, male or female, for me to visit, or with whom I could have any social intercourse, so absolute was my isolation

Flipper, The Colored Cadet at West Point, 1878

How he made it through

Method, not luck

He described the four years as patient endurance and hard and persistent work — lonely and often melancholy, but never surrendered. At graduation, the crowd applauded him.

The mindset

Four working principles

West Point later formalized what he demonstrated in the annual Henry O. Flipper Award, given to a cadet who shows leadership, self-discipline and perseverance through unusual difficulty. These four principles are how that looks in practice.
